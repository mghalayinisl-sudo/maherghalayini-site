Artificial intelligence is moving quickly into higher education. The important question isn’t simply whether colleges should use AI. It’s whether they can use it in ways that make learning clearer, support more students, and help people reach their goals.

That distinction matters. A new tool can be impressive and still add complexity. If students have to navigate more systems, or faculty have to work around technology that doesn’t fit their teaching, adoption alone isn’t progress.

A better starting point is to identify a real need. Where are students getting stuck? Which routine tasks take time away from teaching and advising? What information do people need to make good decisions about their education and careers? AI may help with some of these challenges, but only when it is introduced with purpose.

That purpose should include student success. For example, AI tools might help students practice a concept, get feedback as they work, or find the right campus resource. Faculty and staff might use them to reduce repetitive work and devote more attention to students. In each case, the tool should support human judgment, not replace it.

Responsible use also requires clear expectations. Students and employees need to understand what a tool can and cannot do, what information they should avoid sharing, and when human expertise is essential. Colleges should make space for learning and experimentation while taking privacy, accuracy, accessibility, and fairness seriously.

There is no one-size-fits-all approach. A tool that helps in one course or department may not be useful in another. That’s why listening matters. Students, faculty, staff, and employers can each help identify where technology adds value and where it creates unnecessary friction.

Ultimately, the measure of AI in higher education shouldn’t be how many tools an institution adopts. It should be whether those tools help people learn, teach, and work more effectively. The goal is not technology for its own sake. It is a better educational experience, with more pathways for students to build skills and move toward meaningful opportunities.

That’s the conversation worth having: not just what AI can do, but how we can use it responsibly to help students succeed.
